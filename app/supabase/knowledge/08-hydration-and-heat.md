# Water, heat and Egyptian summers

Egypt is hot for much of the year, and in Upper Egypt and the south
extraordinarily so. Fluid needs rise with temperature, with activity, and with
anything that increases sweating.

There is no single correct number. Two to three litres a day is a reasonable
starting point for an adult in a temperate setting; a labourer outdoors in
Aswan in July may need considerably more. Thirst is a decent guide for most
healthy adults; urine that is pale straw rather than dark is a better one.

## What counts

Water, tea and coffee (caffeine's diuretic effect is small at habitual
intakes), milk, and the water in food — soups, molokhia, watermelon, cucumber,
tomato — all count. What does not help is replacing water with sugary drinks:
sugarcane juice, sweetened karkade and soft drinks add substantial energy while
rehydrating no better than water.

## Salt

In heavy sweating, salt is lost as well as water. For most people the ordinary
Egyptian diet — bread, cheese, pickles, olives — supplies more than enough
without any deliberate addition; the WHO limit of under 5 g of salt a day is
still the target. Salt tablets and electrolyte sachets are for prolonged heavy
exertion or illness with fluid loss, not for daily life.

## Heat and appetite

Appetite falls in heat and rises when it cools, which is one reason evening
eating is heavier in summer. Plan for it rather than being surprised by it:
lighter, water-rich food during the day and the protein at the evening meal.
