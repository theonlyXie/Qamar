# The shape of an Egyptian day

Advice that ignores this gets ignored. A plan built around three evenly spaced
Western meals does not survive contact with a Cairo working day.

## When people eat

- **Breakfast** — early and starchy for anyone working: foul, taameya, eggs,
  cheese, with baladi bread and sweet tea. Often eaten standing or on the way.
- **Lunch** — the main meal, and late. Commonly between **3 and 6 pm**, after
  work and school, and eaten together as a family. This is the largest plate of
  the day by a wide margin: rice, a meat or vegetable stew, salad, bread.
- **Dinner** — light and late, often **9–11 pm**. Cheese, foul, eggs, leftovers,
  fruit, or nothing at all.
- **Tea and snacks** — throughout, and social rather than nutritional.

The practical consequences:

1. **Do not put the largest meal in the evening.** Egyptian lunch is the
   anchor; a plan that makes dinner the main meal is fighting the household's
   schedule, not the person's willpower.
2. **A late dinner is not a problem in itself.** Total intake matters; the
   clock does not. Telling an Egyptian not to eat after 8 pm is asking them to
   skip a meal their family eats together.
3. **The long gap between breakfast and a 5 pm lunch** is where hunger builds
   and where street food gets bought. That gap is the useful place to put a
   planned snack.

## Ordinary household measures

People do not weigh food. These are the units they actually speak in, and
answers should use them.

| Spoken | Roughly |
|---|---|
| رغيف عيش بلدي — one baladi loaf | 90 g |
| طبق وسط — a medium bowl | 400–450 g of koshary or rice-based dish |
| كوباية شاي — a tea glass | 200 ml |
| معلقة كبيرة — a large spoon (tablespoon) | 15 ml / ~15 g |
| معلقة صغيرة — a small spoon (teaspoon) | 5 ml / ~5 g, ~16 kcal of sugar |
| كف — a palmful | ~30 g of nuts or seeds |
| حتة جبنة — a piece of cheese | 30–40 g |
| علبة تونة — a tin of tuna | 120–140 g drained |

Give amounts in these units first and grams second, not the other way round.

## Hospitality, and why "just say no" fails

Refusing food offered by a host is a social act, not a dietary one. Being fed
generously is how affection is expressed, and a guest who declines is
understood to be either ill or offended. "Eat, eat" is not a suggestion.

Advice that works here:

- Eat before going, so a small portion is genuinely enough.
- Accept the plate and leave some — accepting is the part that matters.
- Take the protein and the salad first, and let the bread and the sweet be
  what runs out of room.
- Plan the rest of the day around the invitation rather than trying to refuse
  it.

Advice that does not work: telling someone to decline, or to explain that they
are on a diet. It marks them as difficult and they will stop asking Qamar.

## Cost, and what that means for protein

Meat is expensive and has become more so. A plan that assumes chicken breast
twice a day is a plan for a small minority.

The affordable protein sources that are actually available:

- **Foul and taameya** — the backbone, cheap and everywhere
- **Eggs** — the best value animal protein in the country
- **Gebna qareesh** — high protein, low fat, cheap
- **Lentils** — in koshary, in soup (shorbet ads)
- **Tinned tuna and sardines**
- **Frozen chicken**, cheaper than fresh
- **Milk and laban rayeb**

Build protein targets out of these first. Suggest meat where it fits, not as
the default.

## Bread is not the enemy

Baladi bread is subsidised, wholemeal, and culturally central — it is the
utensil as much as the food. Advising an Egyptian to cut bread is advising them
to eat differently from everyone around them, and it usually fails.

Better: fix what goes on and around it. The oil in the foul, the sugar in the
tea, the fried onion on the koshary and the ghee in the taqleya are larger
terms and are not defended by anybody's grandmother.

## Ramadan, mouled, Eid and the seasons

Food is tied to the calendar and the advice should follow it:

- **Ramadan** — see the Ramadan document; the whole day inverts.
- **Eid al-Fitr** — kahk (biscuits with ghee and sugar), visiting, continuous
  hospitality for several days.
- **Eid al-Adha** — meat in quantity, fattah, liver and offal, for days.
- **Sham el-Nessim** — fesikh (salted fermented mullet), spring onion, boiled
  eggs, lettuce. Note fesikh's very high salt, and that badly prepared fesikh
  is a genuine botulism risk each year — the safety point matters more than the
  calories.
- **Mouled** — sugar dolls and nut-and-sugar sweets (halawet el-mouled).
- **Coptic fasting periods** — extended vegan fasting observed by Coptic
  Christians, who are a large minority. During these, animal products are
  excluded entirely, and protein has to come from foul, lentils, chickpeas,
  tahina and nuts. Never assume a user is Muslim.

Around these, the useful posture is to plan for the day rather than to prevent
it. One heavy day inside a good month changes very little; a month of guilt
loses the user entirely.
