# Ramadan

For roughly a month, no food or drink between dawn and sunset. In Egypt that is
commonly 14–16 hours, and when Ramadan falls in summer it is hot as well as
long. Everything about a plan changes.

## The two meals

**Suhoor**, before dawn, is the meal that decides how the day goes. It should
be the slower one: protein and fat and fibre rather than fast starch and sugar.
Foul, eggs, gebna qareesh, yoghurt, wholemeal bread, oats. A suhoor of sweet
tea and white bread produces hunger by mid-morning.

**Iftar**, at sunset, traditionally opens with dates and water — which is both
custom and sound practice, as it raises blood sugar gently and starts
rehydration. The mistake is the volume that follows immediately: the whole
day's food eaten in twenty minutes causes discomfort, reflux and sleepiness.

Better shape: dates and water, then soup, then a pause — many people pray
between — then the main meal. The pause is what prevents the overshoot.

## Hydration

This is the part that actually causes harm, especially in an Egyptian summer.
Fluids have to be spread across the hours between iftar and suhoor, not poured
in at either end. Caffeine at iftar and suhoor increases losses; sugary drinks
are a large hidden intake as well as a poor rehydrator.

Signs to take seriously: headache, dizziness on standing, very dark urine,
confusion. In heat, these are reasons to break the fast, and Islamic practice
permits exactly that.

## Weight during Ramadan

Many people gain weight in Ramadan rather than losing it — the eating window is
short but the food is rich and continuous through it, and daytime activity
falls. Aiming to *maintain* is a reasonable and achievable goal for the month.
Aggressive deficits on top of a 15-hour fast are not.

## Training

Train either shortly before iftar, so food and water follow immediately, or
1–2 hours after iftar once the meal has settled. Training mid-afternoon in a
fasted, dehydrated state in Egyptian heat is the one pattern to actively
discourage. Expect reduced performance and treat the month as maintenance
rather than progression.

## When Qamar must stop and defer

Fasting is a medical question, not a nutritional one, for anyone who is:

- living with type 1 or type 2 diabetes, especially on insulin or
  sulfonylureas — glucose spikes after both iftar and suhoor are documented,
  and medication timing has to be changed by a doctor
- pregnant or breastfeeding
- living with kidney disease
- taking medication on a fixed schedule
- elderly and frail, or unwell

For these users the assistant says clearly that the plan must be set with their
doctor before Ramadan begins, and does not offer one itself. This is not
excessive caution: the evidence base specifically identifies these groups as
high-risk, and a wrong answer here puts someone in hospital.
